The evidence behind this

Sleep clinicians record “woke up” and “out of bed” as two separate times in the Consensus Sleep Diary, because the gap between them is worth noticing, not judging. A practical idea (not a proven treatment): keep your phone where you have to stand up to switch the alarm off.

On alarm sounds: in one small survey (50 people), melodic alarm sounds were linked to less morning grogginess than harsh beeps (McFarlane et al., 2020), so a gentler tone is a reasonable choice. It is early, self-reported evidence, and I found nothing on bird sounds specifically.

The gentle science

🔬 Support, not another standard.

Why start with just a few minutes?

In short: a little movement counts, and a tiny start is easier to repeat.

Physical activity has health benefits, and some is better than none. Regular activity can support sleep and mood. You do not need a long session for movement to count.

Two minutes here is a low-friction habit starting point, not a proven treatment dose. It does not replace longer-term activity recommendations or guarantee an immediate energy boost. Rebuild repeatability first; add time only if it feels sustainable.

What if motivation never arrives first?

In short: pick a cue, start small, and stop early if you like. You don’t need to feel ready.

Repeating a manageable action after a consistent cue can help it become more automatic. A specific “after X, I will do Y” plan is a behavioral tool—not a promise that starting will always feel easy.

Leave shoes somewhere convenient, choose a familiar safe route, and give yourself permission to stop early. The same route removes a decision. Curiosity missions add optional enjoyment; they are not a clinically validated intervention.

There is no fixed 21-day deadline. Habit formation varies widely, and missing a day is not the same as losing all progress.

Sunshine, light rain, and the winter backup

In short: any outdoor daylight helps your body clock. Skip it when conditions aren’t safe.

Daylight is a timing cue for your body clock. Outdoor light is generally brighter than typical indoor light, even on cloudy days. You do not need direct sunshine, and you should never stare at the sun. Use suitable sun protection.

Light rain need not rule out walking if paths, visibility, clothing, and your health make it safe. Avoid lightning, flooding, slippery ice, dangerous heat or cold, and poor air quality. Choose an indoor version when needed.

In winter, keep the same cue and shorten or move the walk to a safer daylight pocket. If you wake before sunrise, use ordinary indoor lighting and seek daylight when available. This app does not prescribe bright-light therapy or assess weather safety.

Cold-weather checklist

A quick check before a winter walk. If it is extremely cold or windy, the indoor version is a fine choice.

While out: try not to sweat or get wet. Redness or pain on any skin is a cue to go inside or cover it up, since frostbite can start before numbness makes it obvious.

If something feels wrong: shivering that won't stop, confusion, slurred speech or unusual drowsiness can be signs of hypothermia, which is a medical emergency, so get help right away. For suspected frostbite, get warm and seek medical care. Don't rub the skin or use direct heat, and avoid walking on frostbitten feet or toes.

Adapted from CDC winter weather guidance. Older adults, people with long-term health conditions, and people who drink alcohol are at higher risk and may want extra care. CDC: staying safe in the cold (opens online).

Will walking help me sleep earlier and more deeply?

In short: regular activity helps sleep on average, but it can’t promise earlier or deeper sleep.

Regular physical activity can improve sleep quality on average, but it cannot guarantee earlier sleep or more deep sleep for an individual. A consistent wake time, morning daylight, and less bright light in the evening can support an earlier rhythm.

Most adults need about 7–9 hours of sleep. The 15-minute schedule changes in this planner are practical pacing choices, not a validated treatment protocol. Do not keep advancing your alarm if it leaves you chronically short of sleep.

Keep the room comfortably cool, dark, and quiet. Air conditioning is fine if comfortable. If naps delay nighttime sleep, keep them brief and earlier in the day. Never drive when sleepy.

What if I am genuinely exhausted?

In short: rest is allowed. Stop for warning signs, and ask a clinician about ongoing tiredness or low mood.

Rest is a valid choice. Do not exercise through fever, chest pain, dizziness, faintness, or unusual breathlessness. New or severe symptoms may need urgent medical care.

Persistent or unexplained fatigue, activity that leaves you markedly worse afterward, loud snoring or gasping, or sleep problems affecting daily life deserve clinical advice—not a tougher walking goal.

If low mood or loss of interest persists, especially as days shorten, discuss it with a clinician. Persistent insomnia often benefits from CBT-I. The companion is not a therapist or a substitute for assessment.

Evidence & what this app does not measure

The health guidance draws on public-health recommendations and behavioral research. This app itself has not been clinically tested. Its characters, missions, and visual leaves are design features—not medical treatments.

          How to update the app
          1. Save a backup first (Menu → Settings & backup).
          2. Edit index.html on the PC and open it in your browser to try it.
          3. Raise APP_BUILD in index.html by one, so this screen shows which version is live.
          4. Upload only index.html to GitHub: Add file → Upload files → Commit changes. sw.js never needs touching.
          5. Wait about a minute, then refresh the live link. The build number here should be the new one.
          6. If something breaks: on GitHub open index.html → History, and restore the earlier version.
